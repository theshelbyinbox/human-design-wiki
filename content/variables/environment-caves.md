# Environment: Caves

Caves is the first of the six Environments, the values carried by the bottom left arrow of the [[The Four Arrows|four arrows]]. It is not a description of where a person should live on a map. Ra Uru Hu was blunt about this in the lecture where he introduced the six: "I'm not talking about locations. I'm not talking about this city or that city or this country or that country." Environment describes the quality and structure of a space, the shape of the container rather than its address. A cave environment is available in Timbuktu or New York City, in a studio, an office, a bedroom or a car.

The Environment theme sits under a phrase Ra used as the title idea of that lecture: "where you are is who you are." Correct environment is presented not as a preference but as a health matter. Dietary regimen looks after the vehicle from the inside; environment looks after it from the outside, and "provides this nurturing shell. It protects you. It eliminates resistance."

Caves is about enclosure that can be controlled. The defining feature is not darkness or solitude but the doorway, and who decides what passes through it.

## Mechanics

Environment is derived from the COLOUR of the Design Nodes. Caves is colour 1, and as with every variable category the first value is the foundation of the six. Ra: "when we're beginning with any of these constructs, moving from a one to a six ... we're looking at the one because the one is the foundation."

Caves, Markets and Kitchens together form what Ra called the hardscape. "The lower three are our hardscape and the upper three are called landscape." The hardscape environments are built, specific and closed in; the landscape environments of Mountains, Valleys and Shores are, in his word, more generalized. This mirrors the split in dietary regimen, where the lower three colours are conditions and the upper three circumstances. Of the hardscape he said plainly: "It's the hardscape that created the world ... the basic construct of our modern world is all there in the first three environments." Caves is the base of that group, and Ra described cities as an accumulation of it: "Our cities are nothing but caves piled on top of each other."

The arrow beneath Caves points left or right, and that direction comes from TONE, not colour. Ra set out the division of labour: "color establishes what the standard is and tone creates the nuance of that standard bringing it either to a this or a that." Colour tells you the environment is Caves; tone tells you which of its two versions it is. Contemporary teaching labels the two directions of the Environment arrow Observed and Observer, terms that do not appear in Ra's recorded material in this library.

Colour 1 resonates with the first profile line, and Ra drew that connection when explaining why an enclosure suits this colour. "If you think about the first line theme, which is the line of introspection, it is about a fear that the foundation is not solid." See [[line 1|the first line]] for that theme in its own right.

## The Structure of a Cave

Ra built the definition from the architecture outward. "What does a cave environment have? It has only one entry ... At least it has only one obvious entry. There may be a sneaky way out, but there's only one obvious entry. What does that do? That allows you to control the doorway. What does that do for you? Well, it provides you with security."

Control of the single entrance is the mechanism, and everything else follows from it. Ra extended it to children, describing a cave child put in a room with a bedroom door, a bathroom door and a closet door as never comfortable, and guessing that children who wake afraid of monsters are disproportionately cave children. He offered that as an impression, not a finding.

The consequence, he was emphatic, is physical rather than psychological. "It's not just simply that this is a security issue, it's a health issue." Being able to control the door "changes the whole way in which you interact with others ... Not only that, by being in your cave, you are healthier." His practical instruction: "If you get sick, go into a room with one door."

## Caves in Practice

The examples Ra gave are all built, modern and small. The automobile is the one he singled out: "What is the most common cave that we have today? It's the automobile. Cave people love their cars, you know. They close those doors, they lock themselves inside of those cars, they put themselves into movement, and they've got their perfect cave." For work, he added that a cave person with an office needs one with just one door that can be closed.

Contemporary teaching in this library runs along the same line and adds the experience of lacking such a space. A caves person who never knows where they will work or where their things go is described as unsettled and unproductive, and a roving working life is named as a specific friction. The remedy suggested is to build the cave at whatever scale is available: a corner of a small apartment, or the same booth in a shared workspace claimed early each day. The same material keynotes Caves as privacy, control and selective interaction.

## Selective and Blending

The variant names Selective and Blending appear in this library only in contemporary course material, not in Ra's recorded material, and should not be read as his terminology.

In that material, Selective Caves means environments you can control, preferably with your back to a wall, one entrance in and out, calm and quiet with low lighting, living alone or with one other person, and basement workrooms or quiet offices as ideal workspaces. Blending Caves thrives in the same cool, calm spaces but favours more than one entrance or exit, enjoys the throughflow of energy from other people in the space, and is more open to who comes in.

Ra did confirm that binary keynotes exist for each environment, pointing his audience to them on his slide, but he did not read the Caves pair aloud in the material collected here. The words Selective and Blending rest on the contemporary source alone.

## Timing, and the Partner Arrow

Environment is the second of the four transformations, following dietary regimen and preceding [[Perspective]], and Ra placed its relevance late. In an idealized upbringing a child needs only to be treated correctly, to live by its strategy and authority and to follow its dietary regimen, until roughly the Saturn return. "It's at 30 at the Saturn return. This is when the idealized upbringing takes on the demand of environment. It's between 30 and 50 that you establish through the environment its partner perspective and in that develop the foundation for awareness." He told parents not to feel guilty about a child not yet being in its environment.

The pairing is structural. Environment comes from the Design Nodes, Perspective from the Personality Nodes, and Ra treated the second as unreachable without the first: "you cannot take advantage of the unique perspective if you're not in the right place looking at the right things." For a Caves person, the correct view is developed from inside a controlled enclosure, not despite it.

Environment has no transference or distraction mechanic of its own in this corpus. Ra names transference for Motivation and calls the same harmonic pull distraction on the Personality Nodes, that is on Perspective. No equivalent slippage is described for the Design Nodes, and none should be inferred for Caves.
