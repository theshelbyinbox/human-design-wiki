# Environment: Shores

Shores is the sixth and last of the six environments, the settings a body is designed to be nourished by. Ra Uru Hu arrives at it at the end of his run through the sequence with a single sentence: "When we get to the six, we get to shores." What follows in that lecture is not a definition so much as an account of his own life, because Shores was his environment and his partner's.

The word he reaches for is serendipity. "Life's a funny thing. At least it is to me. Serendipity. I like this word serendipity. To be at the right place at the right time." That is the closest thing to a keynote he gives the sixth colour in the Orientation material. The structural reading of Shores, the one most readers meet first, comes from contemporary teaching rather than from Ra directly: transition, transformation and movement, the space where one place becomes another. A shore in this sense is a boundary or an edge, the line along which one thing stops being itself and starts being something else.

Shores is also the best documented of the six, because it is the one whose left and right variant pair Ra names in his own voice. Where the variant names for Caves, Markets and Kitchens survive only in later course material, Ra says plainly what the two sides of Shores are, and says it about himself.

## Mechanics

Environment is read from the colour of the Design Nodes. It occupies the bottom left of the four arrows, on the body side of the chart rather than the mind side. Shores is colour 6, the last value in the sequence that runs Caves, Markets, Kitchens, Mountains, Valleys, Shores.

The arrow's direction is not set by the colour. It is set by the tone sitting underneath it. Ra: "It is tone that establishes whether something is to the left or something is to the right." Tones 1 to 3 give a left-pointing arrow, tones 4 to 6 a right-pointing one. Contemporary teaching labels the two sides of the Environment arrow Observed and Observer; those labels do not appear in Ra's recorded material in this library.

Two further points shape how Environment is read. It sits on the nodal axis, and Ra treats the nodes as the place of meeting: "it's on the nodal line that you meet the other." Being in the correct environment is therefore not only about nourishment but about who a person encounters. And Ra is explicit that environment becomes a health matter later than the other variables: "it's only when you get to the age of 30 and beyond that it is essential for your health that you're in the right environment."

## Natural and Artificial Shores

The variant pair is Natural and Artificial. Ra states it in the first person, in both the Colour lecture and a Gate 16 lecture: "I am somebody who is a shore person, you know, and I am a shore person and I'm here to be on natural shores as opposed to unnatural shores or artificial shores. As long as I'm near the shores, the natural shores, I'm living in an environment that's nurturing me." Note that he uses two words, unnatural and artificial, for the same right-hand side.

There is an inconsistency in the corpus about what decides which side applies, and it is worth flagging rather than smoothing over. In the Orientation lecture Ra describes the general rule: "color establishes what the standard is and tone creates the nuance of that standard bringing it either to a this or a that." On that account the colour gives Shores and the tone gives Natural or Artificial. But in the passage quoted just above, he says the opposite: "it is the color that determines what value in that environment, whether it's going to be natural or whether it's going to be artificial, that is suitable to my vehicle." The two statements come from different lectures and are not reconciled anywhere in this library. The broader Variable framework, and Ra's own tone rule, favour the first.

Later course material fills in what each side describes. A natural shore is a true shore, where water meets land, whether a lake, a stream or an ocean. An artificial shore is the edge between two non-natural environments: a suburb where city blends into country, the border between two countries, a street dividing two neighbourhoods, or a workspace straddling two parts of a house. In both cases what matters is being able to see the two sides meet.

## The Landscape Group and Colour Six

The six environments split three and three. Ra: "there is a difference between the hardscape and the landscape. You know the lower three are our hardscape and the upper three are called landscape." Caves, Markets and Kitchens are the hardscape, and of them he says "it's the hardscape that created the world," the built construct of modern life. Mountains, Valleys and Shores are the landscape.

The difference is one of specificity, the same distinction Ra draws in Determination between conditions and circumstances: "the lower colors are very very specific. They're very specific. But in a sense the upper colors are much more, how can I put this, generalized in that sense. In other words, it's not the closed-in environment, the specific cave, the specific market, the specific kitchen in that sense, but it is a much broader environmental phenomena." Shores sits at the top of that group, the least enclosed and the most transitional of the six.

Colour behaves consistently across the substructure. Ra describes a harmonic running 1 to 4, 2 to 5 and 3 to 6, and the slippage along it under conditioning is what he calls transference. His own worked example uses the sixth colour: "the color is emitting the six. But because the general frequency, the illusion that's being held together by the monopole, because that general frequency is not correct ... instead of getting a six, it pulls in a three." He develops that mechanic for Motivation, where colour 6 is innocence and colour 3 is desire. The corpus does not spell out the Environment version, but the same harmonic would pair Shores with Kitchens. That is an inference from the structure, not a quoted teaching.

## What Shores Looks Like in a Life

Ra's own account is the corpus's clearest picture of what being in or out of a correct environment does. "I was born on an island in the middle of a river and with the exception of two years of my life I have always lived next to water and the two years of my life that I didn't live next to water I was the sickest I have ever been." His partner, also Shores, was seriously ill in the same period. "When I talk about a health issue," he adds, "it's not to be played with."

The nodal side of Environment shows in the same story. He and his partner share the environment and, as he tells it, "we met in that environment. As a matter of fact, we met literally on the shoreline." Their two children are Mountains, which he treats as unremarkable rather than a problem: "environment is not something that is important until that being is ready to live fully their own life as themselves. This is what the first Saturn cycle is about."

## Common Misreadings

The most common error is literalism. Contemporary teachers describing Shores report people insisting they need the beach, and note that a coastline is one instance of a transition, not the definition of one. Ra himself heads this off when introducing the environments: "I'm not talking about locations. I'm not talking about this city or that city or this country or that country." And later: "these environments can be anywhere."

The second is treating Environment as a rule about where to move. Ra's framing is experimental rather than prescriptive: a differentiated environment is "absolutely correct for you," but "until you begin to experiment with that, it's not something that you're going to be able to recognize how profound this truly is." It also arrives late in the chain of transformations, after dietary regimen, and is no substitute for Strategy and Authority.
